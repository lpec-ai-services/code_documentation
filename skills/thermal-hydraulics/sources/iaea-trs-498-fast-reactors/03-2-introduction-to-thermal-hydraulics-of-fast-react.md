# 2. INTRODUCTION TO THERMAL HYDRAULICS OF FAST REACTORS

## 2.1. INTRODUCTION TO FAST REACTORS

Nuclear energy today generates a significant portion of electricity
worldwide, with a small ecological footprint and low carbon emissions. Most
reactors use uranium as fission material to produce electricity. According to
the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development Nuclear Energy
Agency (OECD/NEA) [4], the identified and additional exploitable resources of
uranium are expected to last for another 250 years at the current rate of uranium
use in light water cooled thermal nuclear reactors. By recycling spent fuel in fast
reactors, this length of time could be significantly extended to approximately
5000 years. If, in addition to that, uranium is extracted from seawater, which is
technically feasible but economically not viable at the moment, there is enough
uranium for more than 1 million years.
In fast reactors (see Waltar and Reynolds [5] for a standard textbook on
this topic), the neutrons causing fission are not slowed down to thermal neutrons
in order to increase the probability of creating fission of the uranium isotope
235U. The fraction of this isotope in natural uranium is less than 1%. Instead, in
a fast reactor, the neutrons remain at high energy and have a higher probability
of converting the main uranium isotope 238U to the fissile isotope 239Pu. In this
process, called ‘breeding’, natural uranium can be used much more efficiently.
In a fast reactor design, moderation of neutrons should be avoided. Hence, water
cannot be used as a coolant because it slows down the neutrons. Therefore,
alternative coolants have to be used that are ‘transparent’ for neutrons. Examples
of such coolants include gases (e.g. helium), liquid metals (e.g. sodium,
sodium–potassium alloy, lead or lead–bismuth eutectic (LBE) alloy) and molten
salts (e.g. chloride salts).
In fast reactors, uranium is not only split to produce energy; it also forms
plutonium from non-fissile uranium isotopes, which can also be split to produce
energy. In this case, the breeding ratio is larger than 1, and this is called breeding.
On the other hand, long-lived radioactive elements in the core can be transmuted
into fission products with much shorter half-lives than the original radioactive
elements. This way, the spent fuel from other reactors can be transmuted
to provide energy, while, at the same time, the amount and radiotoxicity of
radioactive nuclear waste can be significantly reduced. When this is done
efficiently, the breeding ratio is typically less than 1, and this is called burning.
Fast reactors can be designed either as burners or breeders. When the reactor is

designed with a breeding ratio close to 1, it means that the reactor is producing as
much fissile material as it consumes. This is often referred to as a self-sustaining
fuel cycle or reactor.

## 2.2. OVERVIEW OF FAST REACTOR DEVELOPMENTS

The experimental breeder reactor EBR-I was the first nuclear reactor
to produce electricity, in 1951. EBR-I was a fast reactor cooled by a
sodium–potassium alloy. Since then, quite a number of fast reactors have been
designed, constructed, operated and decommissioned. Table  1 provides an
overview of the six fast reactors currently in operation worldwide. These reactors
are all sodium cooled and comprise experimental reactors, prototypes and one
commercial reactor.
Table  2 shows that, in addition to the reactors currently in operation,
there are several fast reactors under construction. Three out of four of these
are also sodium cooled, whereas one is cooled by lead. Apart from that, it is
noteworthy to mention that the Prototype Fast Breeder Reactor (PFBR) in India
is in an advanced stage of commissioning and reached criticality in April 2026.
Table 2 also shows fast reactors that have been operated but are currently under
decommissioning. Again, all of these reactors were sodium cooled.

## 2.3. BENEFITS AND DRAWBACKS OF VARIOUS FAST REACTOR

COOLANTS

### 2. 3. 1. Coolant physical and transport properties

Thermal hydraulics is an important aspect of the design of any nuclear
reactor. The various coolants that can be applied in a fast reactor may all have
benefits and drawbacks. Table 4 provides a quick overview of various nuclear
reactor coolants and some of their important properties at approximately
operational conditions. For comparison, the properties of water at 150 bar and
300°C are also mentioned, although water cannot be considered as a coolant
for fast reactors. Properties for water and helium are largely taken from
Lemmon et al. [10]; for sodium, from Fink and Leibowitz [11]; for lead and lead
bismuth, from the OECD/NEA report [12]; and for chloride molten salt, from

<!-- page 9 (PDF 21) -->
Williams [13], assuming magnesium potassium chloride salt (KCl-MgCl2). Some
remarks about the various coolant options are highlighted below.

### 2. 3. 2. Helium

The low boiling point of helium ensures that there are no phase transitions,
while its low density makes it difficult to design compact passive safety systems
based on buoyancy forces. Not mentioned in the properties but of significant
importance is the fact that helium comprises small and lightweight molecules,
resulting in high helium permeability for most materials and seals. Therefore,
it is very difficult to construct a leaktight cooling system using helium. Finally,
a high specific heat supports energy storage in the coolant, increasing the time
to overheating.

### 2. 3. 3. Sodium

The melting point is high and requires the use of preheaters in the cooling
system. The boiling point is such that sodium boiling can be avoided under
operational conditions but needs to be taken into account during accident situations.
If sodium starts boiling, core voiding cannot be avoided, which can affect the neutron
behaviour in the core, increasing its reactivity. A crucial aspect to note is that sodium
is highly chemically reactive with air and water, which can easily lead to fires or
other violent chemical reactions. Therefore, a sodium–water heat exchanger cannot
be used to transfer heat from the primary to the secondary cooling system. A low
Prandtl number is derived from the fact that the ratio of conduction to convective heat
transfer is large, which means that conduction plays a relatively significant role in
heat transfer. This mainly has implications for the modelling of heat transfer, which is
often based on the assumption that the ratio is close to 1.

### 2. 3. 4. Lead and lead–bismuth eutectic

Lead and lead bismuth have high melting points, requiring preheaters for the
cooling system. The high melting point also poses a risk of solidification in accident
situations in which potential flow path blockage cannot be excluded, and this has
to be considered in the safety analysis. On the other hand, the boiling points are
so high that boiling may occur only after the fuel cladding fails. Therefore, core
voiding due to coolant boiling can be excluded, even under accident circumstances.
The high density of lead and lead bismuth allows for the development of compact
passive safety systems based on buoyancy forces. At the same time, the high density
may increase seismic and vibration risks, requiring countermeasures. A low Prandtl
number is indicative of a large ratio between conduction and convective heat transfer,

which means that conduction plays a relatively significant role in heat transfer. This
mainly has implications for the modelling of heat transfer, which is often based on the
assumption that the ratio is close to 1.

### 2. 3. 5. Chloride molten salt

Chloride molten salt, like lead and lead bismuth, has a high melting and
boiling point, leading to similar considerations. The thermal conductivity, on the
other hand, is relatively low, which presents a drawback for heat transfer. A high
Prandtl number is derived from the fact that the ratio of conduction to convective
heat transfer is small, which means that conduction plays a negligible role in heat
transfer. This mainly has implications for the modelling of heat transfer, which is
often based on the assumption that the ratio is close to 1.
In summary, all coolants considered for application in fast reactors have
benefits and drawbacks from an operational and safety point of view.
