## 9.1. EXPLORING LEGACY REACTOR DATA: TOWARDS

COMPREHENSIVE EXPERIMENTAL VALIDATION
The experience and experimental data on LMFRs are based on more than
600 years of SFR operation around the world (e.g. EBR2, Phénix, BN-600,
Rapsodie, Joyo, etc.) [183].

However, the data gained are not available in their entirety for research
activities, or in some cases, the data have been lost. Efforts are needed to
preserve the SFR data and expand it to promote the development of innovative
fast reactors, including LFRs.
Integral test facilities and related data are needed to study multiphysics
effects under relevant conditions, such as unprotected transients, and to analyse
the large scale thermal hydraulics phenomena that reduced scale experiments or
separate effect experiments could not represent.
However, SETs and integral experimental tests on smaller scale facilities
are still necessary to provide more precise data for new codes and to validate new
components with innovative designs [49].
On the new tests, the most advanced instruments of measure could be
implemented, reaching a higher resolution (e.g. LDV/PIV, optical fibres for
temperature, etc.).
R&D activities on instrumentation are necessary not only for thermal
hydraulic analysis; indeed, operating facilities for thermal hydraulics related tests
could be propaedeutic for the testing of new instrumentation in other fields. For
instance, because of the challenging chemical and mechanical conditions to which
materials are exposed in liquid metal cooled reactors [184], new and improved
technologies are needed for maintenance and ex- and in-vessel inspection. The
higher temperatures and the opacity of the coolant create new incentives for
developing technologies.
The licensing of new reactors requires improved numerical codes able to
represent all the aspects of the phenomena involved in the reactor’s life cycle.
The development and validation of reliable and mature thermal hydraulics codes
strongly benefit from high quality experimental campaigns that implement
improved experimental methodologies and instrumentation to acquire the data.
The availability of the obtained data in the international community and the
sharing of state of the art best practices could boost technology development
and provide improved tools for thermal hydraulics analysis in the liquid
metal environment.
The phenomena that show the need for a larger, high quality experimental
dataset cover all possible events in a reactor. Some of these phenomena are
independent of the coolant adopted as the fuel design and fabrication or the fuel
irradiation test. Others, on the other hand, depend on the coolant properties and
the system characteristics, as reported hereafter.

## 9.2. CORROSION AND THE TESTING OF NEW MATERIALS

This issue is shared by SFRs and LFRs; however, experience with heavy
metal coolants is very limited compared with the extensive history of sodium
cooled reactors. For lead and LBE environments, understanding the phenomena
involved (liquid metal embrittlement, oxygen concentration and temperature
dependence, etc.) has made it possible to define reliable conditions under which
some materials can operate. The research is providing new information and
data while looking to demonstrate the possibility of reaching higher coolant
temperatures. This process is strongly enhanced by international collaboration,
thanks to projects such as GEMMA (funded by the European Commission),
which has the scope to investigate the areas where further research and innovation
are needed to reach technological maturity in the frame of the development of
Generation IV reactor materials [185].

## 9.3. FREEZING POINT DURING ACCIDENT CONDITIONS

GIF’s  Safety Design Criteria  [186]  indicates  that “decay heat removal
systems should be designed to prevent overcooling which might lead to freezing of
the coolant”. For this reason, various projects aim to test the passive heat removal
system to ensure this event  is prevented.  At the same time, other projects
investigate the phenomenon to validate thermal hydraulics codes.

## 9.4. CORE REACTIVITY RESPONSE

This point is closely related to thermal hydraulic phenomena and involves
fuel analysis. Different international projects are involved in testing coding
capabilities and studying new solutions, such as the CRP on the analysis of
sodium cooled reactor experiments sponsored by the IAEA on the FFTF [181].

## 9.5. THERMAL STRATIFICATION IN LARGE PLENA

The temperature non-uniformity in large plena could lead to thermal stresses
and fatigue in the structural components. This phenomenon has an impact,
especially in pool type configuration reactors. Facilities such as CIRCE [187]
and E-SCAPE [188] have been adopted to assess the thermal stratification issue
and validate thermal hydraulic codes.

## 9.6. FLOW INDUCED VIBRATION

The flow induced vibrations concern the effect of turbulent fluid motion
on the mechanical integrity of structural materials, in particular in the core.
Conversely, deformations could affect the flow path and the coolability of a
component. International projects such as PASCAL [189] are also going to
investigate the phenomenon.

## 9.7. COOLANT–WATER AND COOLANT–AIR INTERACTION

The coolant’s reactivity with water or air is a fundamental issue that needs
to be studied, especially for SFRs [190]. Even if experiments have already been
done [190], further work could improve understanding of the chemical process
and the predictive capabilities of the codes. For LFRs, experiments on steam
generator tube ruptures are needed in order to fully address machine operability
and assess safety aspects.

## 9.8. PLANT BEHAVIOUR UNDER SEVERE ACCIDENT CONDITIONS

Assessing the reliability of nuclear systems under severe accident
conditions is a key objective of any reactor development project. The sharing of
information in this field could aid in the identification of the most critical issues
and encourage cooperation.
Unlike SFRs, no LFR has been operated yet, and all experience with
these technologies has been gained from experimental facilities. The FALCON
consortium proposed a different strategy for ALFRED’s design, development and
licensing, based on two main paths: the ALFRED research infrastructures and the
ALFRED staged approach.
The construction and operation of dedicated experimental facilities
(ALFRED research infrastructure) [191] are for the purpose of conducting both
separate and integral tests on the reactor components (steam generator, core, etc.)
and their mutual interactions. For instance, it is planned to construct ATHENA
and ELF, two pool type facilities that will investigate single system behaviour
and its mutual interactions (ATHENA) and gain experience in the operation of a
small scale, electrically heated integral facility (ELF). In addition, loop facilities
such as HELENA2 and the corrosion/erosion test facility will complete the
necessary infrastructure to demonstrate the reliability and assess the performance
of the ALFRED system’s design.

The ALFRED staged approach [192] consists of operating the reactor under
increasingly challenging conditions, starting from initial use at low power and
low temperature. At these starting power and temperature values, it has been
demonstrated that the materials operate with wide safety margins and are able to
exclude corrosion during tests conducted at several facilities. From the starting
stage, the power is increased, and the highest temperature is then reached in the
reactor. The experience gained in the previous stage will facilitate the licensing
of the reactor in the following stage. In the reactor, systems will be removable
by design to facilitate maintenance and replacement, and a dedicated test section
will allow for the testing of advanced solutions inside the core. The licensing
process greatly benefits from the staged approach strategy, which justifies the
sustainability of the infrastructure over time and supports the process of acquiring
skills in LFR technology, as well as the necessary data to increase the technology
readiness level, thereby confirming the solutions adopted.
With this approach, it will be possible to move from state of the art operating
conditions to the design conditions of the small modular reactor prototype
regarding power and temperature, demonstrating the viability of LFR technology.
Emerging private startups aim to license their own LFRs in the next decade.
For example, the newcleo reactor design [193] is based on several innovations
that need to be tested and proven. Accordingly, the development programme is
planned to have dedicated infrastructure to deal with the main LFR technological
challenges, including the following:
— Corrosion and erosion, which requires testing of innovative materials and
coatings;
— Opacity of the coolant, which is an issue to be overcome for in-service
inspection and monitoring of the reactor conditions;
— High melting point to guarantee that freezing point is not reached;
— High lead density, which affects seismic design.
The programme anticipates a new experimental campaign on loop and pool
type facilities involving partners such as ENEA Brasimone. A large scale nonnuclear
pool type facility will be built and operated to accurately represent the
newcleo design reactor.
In LFRs, as in SFRs, the sharing of the obtained data could promote the
advancement of the maturity of the technology and fill the gaps in the validation
process of thermal hydraulic codes.
The role of the IAEA is already fundamental to the development of a strong
liquid metal cooled reactor community. IAEA activities such as international
benchmarks [194] and technical meetings promote cooperation and the definition
of best practices in the state of the art of LFR and SFR technologies.

## 9.9. LEGACY DATA

Best practices and the state of the art of the SFR could play a crucial role in
the development of the new LFR community under the coordination of the IAEA.
The results of past international benchmarks could constitute a useful database
of experimental data, and in particular, the resulting ‘guidelines’ on numerical
activities and experimental procedures represent a legacy that needs to be spread
as far as possible among the community.
In the case of SFR technology, a considerable amount of experience was
collected during the operation of Phénix and Superphénix. On the basis of this,
experimental procedures and safety related aspects highlighted during their
operation are to be applied to LFR technology where possible.
Specific databases could have been collected by the following organizations:
— The IAEA itself collects extensive experimental data on various reactor
types [167, 168, 195].
— The US National Nuclear Data Center database [196] includes some
experimental data on nuclear reactions and nuclear structure, though it is
not focused on LFRs.
— Research institutions and universities may have specific databases on their
research topics.
— US national laboratories, such as Oak Ridge National Laboratory, Argonne
National Laboratory and Idaho National Laboratory have extended
databases on sodium technology and SFRs.
— The European Commission Joint Research Centre manages the EU Science
Hub [197], with some data related to fast reactors.
Since these databases constitute the legacy of the nuclear energy experience,
it would be possible to use them to create an ‘open access’ database for fast
reactor technology, facilitating the faster deployment of the knowledge acquired
by all the organizations involved in these projects.

## 9.10. DATA PRESERVATION

The preservation of past and future data is a central task for the fast nuclear
reactor community. This goal could be achieved by means of three important
steps: (a) the proper acquisition of the data, with complete descriptions of the
methodologies used and the conditions under which experiments have been
carried out; (b) the accurate organization of the data to facilitate their readability

and avoid errors due to poor cataloguing; and (c) the eventual sharing of the data,
which is an important step in data preservation.
Sharing of data makes it possible to extend their availability, increasing the
value of the data and facilitating the analysis of the experimental output. In this
way, the quality evaluation of the results is enhanced by comparing them with
similar experimental campaigns, allowing the community to focus on the most
critical and least investigated priorities.

## 9.11. NEED FOR IMPROVED INSTRUMENTATION

Instrumentation is crucial for both R&D programmes and reactor
monitoring and operations. The harsh environment of liquid metal demands
instrumentation that is suitably developed for these applications because of the
coolant properties, as described in Section 2.3. Over the years, much work has
been done to refine the data acquisition system by adopting the most reliable and
accurate instruments [198]; however, more is still needed.
The necessity of testing flow induced vibrations, and therefore the
mechanical behaviour of the structure under the action of flowing coolant,
indicates a requirement for a suitable technology to acquire a proportional signal
to the unknown frequency response of the solid systems.
In Section 5, the importance of CFD codes and 3-D simulations is
highlighted, exposing the lack of instrumentation for accurate velocity and
temperature measurements inside complex systems. The opacity of the coolant
hinders the adoption of any kind of optical based instrumentation, requiring the
application of other approaches to solve the problem.
In addition to the velocity, the measurement of the mass flow rate could
also be problematic, especially under conditions of strong variation in the mass
flow values during the same experiment, such as could be found in a forced to
natural circulation test. Currently, various instruments are adopted, but there is no
clear winner among the available technologies.
Eventually, for new fast reactor concepts, the reliability of instrumentation
for the monitoring of neutron flux and temperature during normal and abnormal
operation has to be demonstrated. Continuing measurement of the outlet
temperature of the core fuel assemblies could ensure the detection of the
formation of blockages, preventing the initiation of a severe accident. However,
the monitoring system has to be integrated into the complexity of the reactor.
Research efforts on the development of wireless sensing could address the need
for new technologies in this application.
Overall, the field needs further improvements in the available technologies,
not only for LMFR development, but also for other coolants. Only with accurate

analysis of critical phenomena be closed.

## 9.12. PRIORITIES

As stated at the beginning of this section, knowledge of many aspects of
LFR technology still needs to be deepened. Many topics have been covered
by various experiments over the past few years through suitable experimental
facilities, bridging the gap between experiments and theory. Nevertheless, there
are examples of phenomena involved in both normal and accident conditions that
require additional efforts to prioritize their understanding and analysis.
For instance, the thermal hydraulic behaviour of fuel assemblies has
been investigated in several experiments involving bare and wire wrapped fuel
bundles of different dimensions in a heavy liquid metal environment. However,
the combined effects of further experiments and improved instrumentation
could produce better correlations than the current ones in terms of heat transfer
coefficient and pressure drops. Moreover, the phenomenon of flow induced
vibration needs to be carefully addressed in order to characterize the fuel element
from a hydraulic mechanical point of view, making it possible to predict its
behaviour during accident conditions.
Moving to the steam generator, phenomena like steam generator tube
rupture are a safety concern because the very high pressure compared with the
primary side causes the flashing of the steam–water mixture in the primary
vessel. The pressure wave propagation, and an eventual entrainment of vapour
inside the core, are key aspects in the design of the reactor and in the analysis of
accident evolution.
An accidental injection of cold water into the primary vessel or an
overcooling of the feedwater could cause solidification of the primary coolant,
leading to flow obstruction and flow rate reduction in the core. The propagation
of the solidification front is a relevant safety concern, and its physics requires
a comprehensive understanding and suitable numerical models to study the
phenomenon from a numerical point of view.
Material testing is one of the most critical aspects in an LFR because the
compatibility of the reactor materials with the lead could be an issue even during
normal operation. Corrosion effects are especially important at high temperatures,
which are needed in order to make the LFR competitive in the market.
In general, integral effect facilities are constructed and operated in order
to test materials, components and prototypes (e.g. fuel assembly design, steam
generators, etc.) and to study the effects of neutron irradiation on material and
component performance.
